Silverleaf is one of the best-known private golf addresses in Scottsdale, and its club is one of the most expensive to join. If you are pricing a membership, the first thing to know is that the club does not publish its fees. Every number you will find online, including the ones in this article, comes from third-party reporting.

This guide pulls those published estimates together, shows the ranges side by side, and walks through the costs that most estimates leave out. It also lists the questions you should ask the club before you commit.

*Important: This is general editorial information, not legal, tax or financial advice. Club fees and terms change, often every year. Verify every figure with Silverleaf Club directly and review the membership documents with a licensed real estate, legal or financial professional before you make a decision.*

## What Silverleaf Club is

Silverleaf Club sits inside the Silverleaf community in North Scottsdale, within the larger DC Ranch area and alongside the McDowell Sonoran Preserve. The community was developed by DMB. The club’s golf course is a Tom Weiskopf design that opened in 2002. It is an 18-hole, par-72 private course that plays a little over 7,300 yards from the back tees.

Silverleaf’s reputation rests on three things: a private, low-density setting at the edge of the preserve, strict architectural standards across the community, and a club built around a single championship course rather than a large multi-course campus. That combination is a big part of why its membership is priced at the top of the Scottsdale market.

## The two membership tiers

Silverleaf Club offers two tiers of membership.

**Golf Membership.** This is full membership. It includes the golf course along with the club’s dining, fitness, spa, pool and social facilities.

**Clubhouse Membership.** This tier covers dining, pools, fitness, the spa and the social calendar, but not regular golf. It suits households that want the club’s social life without paying for golf they will not use, and it can be a way to join the club while waiting for a Golf Membership to open up. One source describes limited golf access for Clubhouse members during the summer months. Ask the club whether that applies today and on what terms.

The gap between the two tiers is large, so the most important decision is whether golf access is truly worth it for your household. If one partner plays several times a week and the other never plays, it is worth asking the club what household and family options exist within each tier.

## What published sources say Silverleaf costs

Here are the most recent published estimates we found. None of them come from the club.

**Golf Membership**

- **Initiation:** roughly $400,000 to $500,000 in 2026 estimates. Reporting from 2024 cited about $400,000.
- **Monthly dues:** roughly $2,750 to $3,900.

**Clubhouse Membership**

- **Initiation:** about $100,000.
- **Monthly dues:** roughly $875 to $900.

**A note on old numbers.** You will still find older listings quoting a Golf initiation of $150,000 and dues around $1,500 a month. Those figures are years out of date. Initiation fees at top Scottsdale clubs have risen sharply, and an old number can throw off a budget by hundreds of thousands of dollars. Always check the date on any figure you rely on.

**A note on waitlists.** Reporting from 2024 described a multi-year waitlist for Golf Memberships. If that is still true when you apply, the price you are quoted today may not be the price you pay when a spot opens up. Ask whether initiation is locked when you join the list or set when you are admitted.

## What you actually pay in year one

The initiation is the headline, but it is only part of the bill. Here is how to build a realistic first-year number, using the published ranges above.

**Dues.** At roughly $2,750 to $3,900 a month, Golf Membership dues come to about $33,000 to $46,800 a year. Clubhouse dues at roughly $875 to $900 a month come to about $10,500 to $10,800 a year.

**Food and beverage minimums.** Many private clubs require members to spend a minimum amount on dining each year, whether or not they use it. Ask whether Silverleaf has one, how much it is, and whether unused amounts are forfeited.

**Assessments.** Clubs raise money for big projects, such as course renovations or clubhouse upgrades, through assessments charged to members. Ask whether any assessments are planned or currently being paid, and how the amount is decided.

**Capital or reserve charges.** Some clubs add a recurring capital charge on top of dues to fund long-term maintenance. Ask whether this exists and whether it is included in the quoted dues.

**Golf-related charges.** Ask about cart fees, locker fees, bag storage, guest fees and caddie or forecaddie fees. These add up quickly for members who play often or host guests.

**Taxes and service charges.** Ask whether dues and dining are subject to tax and whether a service charge is added to food and beverage.

Putting it together, a Golf member in the first year pays the initiation, plus roughly $33,000 to $47,000 in dues, plus whatever minimums, assessments and charges apply. That is before the cost of owning a home in Silverleaf or nearby. The club can give you the actual figures for each line. Ask for them in writing.

## The questions that matter more than the price

Two clubs with the same initiation can be very different deals. These questions tell you what your money actually buys.

**Who owns the club?** We could not confirm Silverleaf Club’s ownership structure from public sources. Ask directly. In a member-owned (equity) club, members collectively own the club and usually elect the board that sets dues and assessments. In a club owned by a company or individual, your initiation buys the right to use the club, and the owner makes the big decisions. Our [equity vs. non-equity primer](/blog/scottsdale-golf-club-equity-vs-non-equity) explains the difference.

**Is any part of the initiation refundable?** Some clubs return part of the initiation when your membership is resold. Others return nothing. Ask what happens to your initiation if you resign, sell your home or move away, and how long a refund would take.

**Is membership tied to a home?** Ask whether you must own property in Silverleaf to join, and whether a membership can transfer to the buyer when you sell. The answer affects both your purchase and your eventual resale.

**How are dues increases decided?** Ask for the history of dues increases over the last several years. That tells you more about your future costs than this year’s number does.

**What does the club plan to build?** A major renovation can mean a large assessment. Ask about the capital plan for the next five to ten years.

**What is the current waitlist?** Ask how long the wait is for a Golf Membership, how the list is ordered, and whether a deposit is required to hold a place.

## How Silverleaf compares with other Scottsdale clubs

Published estimates place Silverleaf’s Golf initiation at or near the top of the Scottsdale market. For context, published 2026 estimates for Scottsdale-area private clubs generally range from roughly $35,000 to $350,000 or more for initiation, with monthly dues from roughly $800 to $3,500. Silverleaf’s estimated Golf initiation sits above the top of that general range.

The comparison that matters most is not the price, though. It is the model. Silverleaf is a single-course club in a low-density community. Desert Mountain, by contrast, has seven courses and a much larger membership. Whisper Rock is a golf-only club with no homes on site. Each model suits a different kind of member. Our [Silverleaf vs. Whisper Rock comparison](/blog/silverleaf-vs-whisper-rock-2026) covers the trade-offs in detail.

## Who Silverleaf membership makes sense for

A Silverleaf Golf Membership tends to make sense for members who:

- Play often enough that the annual dues work out to a reasonable cost per round
- Want a smaller, quieter club culture built around one course
- Live in or near Silverleaf and will use the dining and social calendar regularly
- Plan to stay long enough for the initiation to make sense over time

It tends to be a poor fit for members who:

- Play occasionally and would be better served by a Clubhouse Membership or a less expensive club
- Want variety, with several courses to rotate through
- May move within a few years and have not confirmed what, if anything, they would get back on resignation

## How to approach the decision

Start with how you will actually use the club, not with the price. Count how many rounds you realistically play in a year, how often you would eat at the club, and whether anyone else in your household would use the fitness, spa or social programs. Then compare the annual cost against that use.

Next, get the documents. Ask the club for the membership plan or bylaws, the current fee schedule, the dues history and any information on planned assessments. Have a real estate attorney or financial advisor review them with you, especially the sections on refunds, transfers and assessments.

Finally, treat the initiation as a long-term decision. A six-figure initiation spread over ten years of regular use is a very different proposition from the same amount spread over three. If you are buying a home at the same time, decide on the club before you remove your purchase contingencies.
