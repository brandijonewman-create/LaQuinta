“Equity membership” is one of those phrases that gets used constantly in golf real estate and explained rarely. Most explanations stop at the definition: members own the club. That is true, but it is the least useful part. What matters to a buyer is how that ownership actually works over the life of a membership, from the day you join to the day you leave.

Our [equity vs. non-equity primer](/blog/scottsdale-golf-club-equity-vs-non-equity) covers how to choose between the two models, and our [club-by-club guide](/blog/equity-vs-non-equity-golf-memberships-scottsdale-clubs) covers which Scottsdale clubs use which. This article goes one level deeper and walks through the mechanics.

*Important: This is general editorial information, not legal, tax or financial advice. Every club’s documents are different, and the documents control. Review any membership plan, bylaws and related agreements with a licensed real estate, legal or financial professional before you join.*

## The short definition

An equity membership is a club membership that includes a share of ownership in the club. The members collectively own the club, usually through a member-owned corporation, and elect a board of directors to run it. Your initiation buys two things at once: the right to use the club and your share of its ownership.

A non-equity membership, by contrast, buys only the right to use the club. A company, developer or individual owns it and makes the major decisions.

## The documents that define your membership

At an equity club, your rights and obligations are written down in a set of governing documents. The names vary, but you will usually see some combination of:

- **Articles of incorporation**, which create the member corporation
- **Bylaws**, which set out how the club is governed, how directors are elected and how members vote
- **A membership plan or membership program**, which defines the membership categories, the initiation, refund and transfer rules, and the obligations of members
- **Rules and regulations**, which cover day-to-day matters such as tee times, guests and dress codes
- **A membership agreement or application**, which you sign when you join

The membership plan is usually the most important document for a buyer, because it contains the financial terms. It is also the document most often amended over time, which means the terms that apply to you may change after you join. Ask how the plan can be amended and who has to approve changes.

## Step one: joining

Joining an equity club usually follows a similar path:

1. **Application.** You apply, often with sponsorship from existing members, and the club reviews your application.
2. **Waitlist.** If the membership category you want is full, you may join a waitlist. Some clubs ask for a deposit to hold your place. One club’s published bylaws, for example, require a non-refundable option fee that is credited toward the initiation when a spot opens. Ask whether any deposit is refundable if you change your mind.
3. **Initiation.** When a spot opens, you pay the initiation. At an equity club this is typically described as an equity contribution or the purchase of a membership certificate or share.
4. **Dues and ongoing charges begin.** From that point you pay dues, and any minimums or assessments that apply.

One detail to check early is whether the initiation is fixed when you join the waitlist or set when you are admitted. At a popular club, the difference can be significant.

## Step two: governing the club

Ownership comes with a vote. At most equity clubs, members elect the board, and the board makes most decisions. Larger decisions, such as major capital projects, changes to the bylaws or the sale of the club, usually require a member vote. The bylaws set the thresholds.

In practice, this gives members real influence over three things that affect their costs:

- **Dues.** The board sets dues each year, usually as part of an annual budget.
- **Capital projects.** Course renovations, new clubhouses and other major projects are proposed by the board and often approved by members.
- **Assessments.** When a project or shortfall needs funding, the club may charge members an assessment, either as a lump sum or spread over several years.

The trade-off is that control and cost go together. Because members own the club, members pay for it. If the club decides to rebuild its clubhouse, every member shares the bill, whether or not they voted for it.

## Step three: leaving, and what you get back

This is where equity memberships vary most, and where buyers most often misunderstand what they are buying.

**Refund models.** There is no industry standard. Common approaches include:

- **A percentage refund.** The club returns a fixed share of your equity contribution when your membership is resold. One Florida club’s documents, for example, set refunds at 80% of the equity contribution value at the time of resale.
- **A refund tied to the current price.** Some clubs refund based on what a new member pays at the time your membership is resold, which can be more or less than what you paid.
- **Member-set resale.** Some clubs let departing members sell their memberships, sometimes at a price they set, subject to club approval.
- **No refund.** Some equity clubs return nothing. Ownership gives you a vote and a share in the club, but not a cash value on departure.

**Resale queues.** Many equity clubs pay refunds only when a membership is resold to a new member. Resigned members wait in a queue, and each new member’s initiation funds a refund for someone at the front. Some clubs use a ratio, such as one resale for every new membership sold, to manage the queue. When demand is strong, the wait may be short. When demand is weak, resigned members can wait a long time.

**Transfer fees.** Clubs commonly keep part of the value when a membership changes hands. One industry source puts typical transfer fees at roughly 10% to 20% of the membership’s value. That is why even a “fully refundable” membership rarely returns the full amount.

**Continuing obligations.** At some clubs, resigned members must keep paying dues until their membership is resold. Ask whether this applies, because it can turn a long resale queue into a real cost.

## Step four: selling your home

If the club sits inside a residential community, selling your home raises a second set of questions. Depending on the club:

- The membership may transfer to the buyer with the home, often with a transfer fee
- You may resign and enter the resale queue, while the buyer applies separately and pays their own initiation
- Membership may be required for owners in the community, or entirely optional

The answers can be in the club’s documents, the community’s CC&Rs, or both. Ask before you list the home, because they can affect both your price and your timeline.

## When the club itself changes hands

Equity clubs can be bought, sold or restructured. The best-documented local example runs in the opposite direction from what most people expect. In 2011, Desert Mountain’s members bought their club’s six golf courses, related facilities and about 500 acres of developable land from Crescent Real Estate Holdings for a reported $73.5 million, turning a developer-owned club into a member-owned one.

If an equity club is sold, merged or converted, the club’s documents and a member vote usually govern what happens, including how any proceeds are distributed and what happens to outstanding refunds. These are rare events, but they are exactly the moment when the details in your documents matter most. Ask how a sale would be approved and what it would mean for your equity.

## Common misunderstandings

**“Equity means I get my money back.”** Not necessarily. Equity means ownership. Whether ownership comes with a refund depends on the documents.

**“Equity means lower costs.”** Not necessarily. Members carry the full cost of running and improving the club, including assessments.

**“Equity is an investment.”** It is generally better to think of an equity membership as a lifestyle purchase with some potential to recover part of the cost. A licensed financial or tax professional can tell you how it fits your situation.

**“The terms I sign are the terms I keep.”** Membership plans can be amended. Ask how changes are made.

## Questions to ask any equity club

Before you join, ask for the governing documents and get written answers to these questions:

1. Who owns the club, and how is the member corporation structured?
2. What does my initiation buy: a certificate, a share or another form of equity?
3. Is any part of the initiation refundable? How is the refund calculated?
4. Are refunds paid only on resale? How long is the current resale queue?
5. What transfer fee applies when a membership changes hands?
6. Do I keep paying dues after I resign and before my membership is resold?
7. How are dues increases and assessments approved? Are any planned?
8. Does the membership transfer with a home sale, or does the buyer apply separately?
9. How can the membership plan be amended, and who must approve changes?
10. What happens to my equity if the club is sold or restructured?

Review the answers with a licensed real estate, legal or financial professional before you sign anything.
