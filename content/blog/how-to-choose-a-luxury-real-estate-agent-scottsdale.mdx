A golf home in Scottsdale is two purchases in one: the property and the club life that comes with it, or doesn’t. The right agent understands both. They know which communities include golf and which sell memberships separately, which clubs have wait lists, and how the Arizona contract protects you while you sort it all out.

This guide covers how to verify an agent, what has changed in buyer representation, how the Arizona purchase process works, and the questions that separate a luxury specialist from a generalist.

*Important: This is general editorial information, not legal or financial advice. Licensing rules, contract forms and industry practices change. Verify every requirement and term with the Arizona Department of Real Estate, your agent and a licensed real estate attorney before you sign anything.*

## Step one: verify the license

Every person who represents you in an Arizona real estate transaction must be licensed by the Arizona Department of Real Estate (ADRE). The department runs a free public database, and it is the first place to look.

A license record on the database shows:

- License type (salesperson or broker) and status
- Original license date and expiration date
- The current employing brokerage and its designated broker
- Employment history
- Recent continuing education courses
- Whether there are open complaints, and any orders on file

Two things to check closely. First, the **original license date** tells you how long the agent has been licensed, which is a useful starting point for conversations about experience. Second, the **complaints** section. The department notes that open complaints represent unproven allegations, so read them as a prompt for a question, not a verdict.

**REALTOR versus licensee.** REALTOR is a trademark for members of the National Association of REALTORS. Every agent must hold a state license, but not every licensee is a REALTOR. The ADRE makes this point to consumers directly.

## Step two: understand the buyer agreement

Buyer representation changed nationally in 2024. Under practice changes that followed a settlement by the National Association of REALTORS, effective **August 17, 2024**, agents working with buyers through an MLS must enter a written agreement with the buyer before touring a home. That applies to in-person tours and live virtual tours. Speaking with an agent at an open house, or asking about their services, does not require one.

The written agreement must state, in conspicuous language, that broker commissions are not set by law and are fully negotiable. It also sets out what the agent will do for you and how they will be paid.

The ADRE encourages consumers to read and fully understand any written agreement they sign with a licensee. Before you sign, ask:

- How long does the agreement last?
- Is it exclusive, and does it cover all homes or a defined area?
- How is the agent paid, and what happens if the seller offers compensation?
- How can either side end it?

A confident agent will walk you through the agreement line by line and will not rush you.

## Step three: know how the Arizona process works

Most Arizona resale purchases use the **Arizona REALTORS Residential Resale Real Estate Purchase Contract**. A good agent knows it in detail. These are the parts that matter most to buyers.

### The inspection period

The contract’s default inspection period is **10 days after contract acceptance**, unless the parties write in a different number. During that window you complete your inspections and investigations: the home inspection, the roof, the pool, insurance availability, HOA documents and anything else that matters to you.

For a large golf property with a pool, outdoor kitchen, casitas and complex systems, 10 days goes quickly. Your agent should line up inspectors before you are under contract, and may negotiate a longer period if the home calls for it.

### The SPDS

The contract requires the seller to deliver a completed **Seller’s Property Disclosure Statement (SPDS)** within three days after acceptance. The SPDS covers the seller’s knowledge of the property: ownership, the HOA, the structure, systems, water, environmental issues and more. If the SPDS arrives late, the buyer has the inspection period or five days after receiving the SPDS, whichever is later, to give notice of items disapproved.

Read the SPDS slowly, with your agent. The details it reveals often shape what you inspect.

### The BINSR

The **Buyer’s Inspection Notice and Seller’s Response (BINSR)** is the form a buyer uses during the inspection period to list items they disapprove of, and the seller uses to respond. The BINSR requires the buyer to acknowledge that all desired inspections were completed before the notice was delivered. Timing and precision matter here, and this is where an experienced agent earns their fee.

### HOA and club documents

In a golf community, you may be reviewing documents from a master HOA, a sub-association and a club, each with its own rules and fees. Your agent should know which documents to request, how long they usually take to arrive, and what to look for.

## Step four: test their golf knowledge

A luxury agent in Scottsdale should be fluent in the club side of the purchase. Ask them to explain:

- **Which communities include club access and which do not.** In many communities, the home and the club are separate purchases.
- **Membership categories.** Full golf, social and other levels, and what each includes.
- **Equity versus non-equity clubs.** How each works and what happens to your membership when you sell. Our [equity membership guide](/blog/equity-vs-non-equity-golf-memberships-scottsdale-clubs) explains the difference.
- **Transfer rules.** Whether a membership can transfer with the home, and on what terms.
- **Wait lists.** Which clubs have them, and how they affect timing.
- **Initiation and dues.** How to get current figures directly from the club.

An agent who answers these clearly, and tells you to confirm the details with the club in writing, is an agent who has done this before.

## The agent interview checklist

Interview at least two agents. These questions are worth asking each one:

1. **License.** What is your license type, and which brokerage do you work under?
2. **Focus.** What share of your recent transactions were in the communities and price range we are considering?
3. **Recent sales.** Can you walk us through two recent sales similar to the home we want?
4. **Golf knowledge.** How do memberships work in the communities we are considering?
5. **Buyer agreement.** Can you walk us through your agreement, including term, exclusivity, compensation and how to end it?
6. **Inspections.** Which inspectors do you use for luxury homes with pools and complex systems, and can you have them ready in time?
7. **Off-market homes.** How do you find homes that are not publicly listed, and how often does that happen in our price range?
8. **Part-year buyers.** How do you handle a purchase when we are out of state for part of the process?
9. **Team.** Will we work with you directly, or with team members?
10. **Communication.** How and how often will you update us?
11. **References.** Can we speak with two recent clients?
12. **Next step.** What would you recommend we do first?

## If you are selling, the questions change

Many buyers in Scottsdale are also sellers, either of a home elsewhere or of the golf home they are leaving for another community. When you interview a listing agent, focus on how they will reach the buyer most likely to pay for what your home offers:

- **Pricing.** How will they price the home, and which recent comparable sales support it?
- **The club story.** Can they explain clearly what a buyer gets, or does not get, with the membership?
- **Presentation.** How will they show the view, the course frontage and the outdoor living spaces in photos and video?
- **Reach.** How do they reach part-year and out-of-state buyers, who are a large share of the golf home market?
- **Disclosure.** How will they help you complete the SPDS fully and accurately? A careful disclosure protects you after closing.
- **Commission.** Commissions are negotiable. Ask what is included, and how buyer agent compensation will be handled under current practice.

Verify the listing agent’s license on the ADRE database, just as you would a buyer’s agent.

## Timing your search

Scottsdale’s market follows the seasons. Many buyers search in winter, when part-year residents are in town and the weather is at its best, while others prefer quieter months. Our [guide to the best month to buy](/blog/best-month-to-buy-in-scottsdale) covers the trade-offs. A good agent will tell you honestly how timing affects your search in your price range.

## The bottom line

Choose the agent who can explain the Arizona contract, the buyer agreement and the club rules in plain language, and who has recently closed sales like yours in the communities you love. Then verify the license yourself on the ADRE database before you sign.
