Many of the best golf homes in Scottsdale were built in the 1990s and 2000s, when the area’s major golf communities were taking shape. They sit on lots that cannot be replaced, with views that do not change. What does change is how people want to live in them: bigger glass, larger kitchens, indoor-outdoor rooms, modern systems and updated finishes.

A remodel is often the fastest path from a great location to the home you want. This guide covers what to know before you hire: who can legally do the work, when permits apply, how community design review works, and how to protect yourself in the contract.

*Important: This is general editorial information, not legal, financial or construction advice. Licensing rules, city codes, community guidelines and costs change. Verify every requirement for your project with the relevant agency and a licensed professional before you start.*

## Who can legally do the work

Arizona licenses contractors through one statewide agency, the **Arizona Registrar of Contractors (ROC)**. As a general rule, work valued at $1,000 or more, or any job that requires a building permit, needs a licensed contractor. Small handyman jobs below that line may not, but a remodel of a golf home will almost always be above it.

For a remodel, the classifications you are most likely to see are:

- **B-3, General Remodeling and Repair Contractor.** The general classification for remodels and repairs.
- **B, General Residential Contractor.** Broader residential building, including additions.
- **B-5 and B-6, Swimming Pool Contractor.** For pool remodels and new pools.
- **Specialty "R" classifications.** For specific trades, often hired by the general contractor.

### How to check a contractor

The ROC publishes a free license search. Before you sign, confirm:

1. The license is **active**.
2. The **classification covers your project**.
3. The **business name on the license matches** the company on your contract.
4. The **complaint history** shows nothing that worries you, and any past complaints were resolved.

The ROC also has rules about displaying license numbers in advertising. A contractor who will not give you a license number is a contractor to skip.

### Why licensing protects you

Licensed residential and dual contractors pay into the **Residential Contractors’ Recovery Fund**. The ROC describes it as financial protection that licensed residential contractors provide to residential homeowners. If a licensed residential contractor violates the licensing law and damages you, the fund may compensate you, within its limits.

The fund has a process. A homeowner must first file a claim against the contractor’s license bond and get a final determination before filing a fund claim, and there are deadlines. None of this is available if you hire an unlicensed contractor. *Verify current rules with the ROC or a construction attorney.*

## Permits: what the city needs to see

The City of Scottsdale’s Planning and Development Services handles building permits. As a general guide:

- **Usually needs a permit:** structural changes such as removing walls, additions, new windows or door openings, most plumbing, electrical and mechanical work, new pools and major pool changes.
- **Usually does not:** painting, flooring replacement and cabinet replacement without plumbing or electrical changes.

Every project is different, so confirm with the city. Then put permit responsibility in your contract: who applies, who pays the fees, who schedules inspections, and who fixes anything that fails.

**Why permits matter beyond the law.** Unpermitted work can cause trouble when you sell. Buyers’ inspectors and agents look for it, and Arizona’s seller disclosure form asks about past work and permits. Permitted work with passed inspections is easier to sell.

## Native plants and the landscape

If your remodel reaches the yard, such as a new pool, an outdoor kitchen, a larger patio or new landscaping, Scottsdale’s **Native Plant Ordinance** may apply. It covers the entire city and protects many large native cacti and trees. Removing, relocating or destroying a protected plant generally requires a native plant permit.

Homes inside the city’s **Environmentally Sensitive Lands** overlay, common in north Scottsdale, may also carry **Natural Area Open Space** easements that limit what you can change. Before you design an outdoor project, check whether the lot has NAOS, and where.

## Community design review

Most of Scottsdale’s gated golf communities have a design review committee and written design guidelines. For a remodel, expect review of:

- Exterior changes: windows, doors, roofing, paint colors and stone
- Additions and changes to the home’s footprint
- Pools, patios, outdoor kitchens and fire features
- Lighting, landscaping and walls

Many communities also regulate **construction hours, contractor access, parking and staging**. Golf course lots often have extra rules about work near the course and protecting views.

**Plan the timeline.** Design review typically comes before the city permit, and committees meet on a schedule. Ask for the submittal calendar early, and build it into your project timeline. ## Scheduling around the seasons

Many part-year owners want the work done while they are away in summer. That can be a smart choice for interior work, since you avoid living through the dust and noise. But summer in Scottsdale brings challenges:

- **Heat** affects crews, working hours and productivity.
- **Some materials,** such as certain concrete, adhesives and finishes, have temperature limits for installation.
- **Monsoon storms** can interrupt outdoor work from early summer into fall.

Ask your contractor how they plan for summer, and which parts of your project are best done in cooler months.

## Remote owners: managing from away

If you will be out of town during the work:

- Agree in writing on **who can approve changes** while you are away.
- Ask for **photo or video updates** on a set schedule.
- Consider hiring an **owner’s representative** or asking your architect or designer to visit the site.
- Coordinate with your **community’s gate and security** on contractor access.
- Make sure your **homeowners insurance** is informed about the renovation.

## What to put in the contract

A written contract protects both sides. It should include:

1. **Scope of work,** in detail, with plans and specifications attached.
2. **Price and payment schedule,** tied to completed milestones rather than dates.
3. **Allowances,** with each amount and what it covers.
4. **Change order process,** including how changes are priced and approved in writing.
5. **Schedule,** with start and completion dates and how delays are handled.
6. **Permits and inspections,** with responsibility clearly assigned.
7. **HOA compliance,** including who submits for design review.
8. **Insurance,** with certificates for general liability and workers’ compensation.
9. **Lien protection.** Ask about lien waivers from the contractor and major subcontractors as you pay.
10. **Warranty,** in writing, with what is covered and for how long.
11. **Cleanup and site protection,** including how the course side of the lot is protected.

Have an attorney review the contract on a large project.

## Budgeting a remodel

Remodel costs depend on scope, the age and condition of the home, the finishes you choose and what the walls reveal once they are open. We do not quote a single figure because the range is too wide to be useful. Instead:

- Get **at least two or three detailed bids** for the same scope.
- Compare **allowances line by line,** not just totals.
- Hold a **contingency** for surprises in older homes.
- Ask each contractor what they **commonly find** in homes of your home’s age.

*Verify all pricing with licensed professionals for your specific project.*

## Projects that suit golf homes

Owners of established golf homes tend to focus on a familiar set of projects. Each has its own considerations:

- **Opening to the view.** Larger windows and sliding or folding glass walls are popular on course and mountain view lots. They usually involve structural work, permits and design review.
- **Kitchens.** Larger islands, better ventilation and more storage are common goals. Expect plumbing, electrical and gas changes.
- **Primary suites.** Larger showers, soaking tubs and walk-in closets often mean moving walls and plumbing.
- **Outdoor living.** Covered patios, outdoor kitchens, fire features and shade structures extend the season. They often involve design review and may touch native plant rules.
- **Pools.** Remodels range from new finishes to full redesigns. A licensed pool contractor is required.
- **Systems.** Updated cooling, water treatment, lighting control and security can matter more than finishes in a home that sits empty part of the year.
- **Golf spaces.** Simulator rooms, putting greens and club storage rooms are increasingly common.

## Contractor interview checklist

1. What is your ROC license number and classification?
2. How many remodels have you completed in this community?
3. Can we see a recent project and speak with the owner?
4. Who will manage our job day to day?
5. Who pulls the permits, and who handles design review submittals?
6. How do you price change orders?
7. How do you protect the home, the neighbors and the course during work?
8. How do you plan for summer heat and monsoon season?
9. What warranty do you provide, in writing?
10. How will you keep us updated if we are away?
