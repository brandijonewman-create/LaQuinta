Moving to Scottsdale for golf is easy. Finding people to play with every week can take a little more effort. Whether you are new to the area, newly retired or simply want to play more, a regular golf group makes the game more social, more competitive and easier to fit into your week.

This guide is for residents and newcomers looking for a regular group. If you are planning a one-time trip or outing for a group, see our [guide to planning Scottsdale golf for groups](/blog/scottsdale-golf-group-trip-planning-guide).

*Important: This is general editorial information, not legal, tax or financial advice. Group details, fees and policies change. Confirm current information directly with each course, association or club, and consult a licensed professional on any club membership or home decision.*

## The main types of golf groups

**Golf associations at public courses.** Many courses host men’s, women’s or mixed associations. Members typically pay annual dues, play on set days and take part in regular competitions. These are among the most common ways newcomers find a weekly game.

**Leagues.** Some courses and organizations run leagues, often weekly, with team or individual formats. Some rotate between courses.

**Private club games.** Private clubs run their own regular games, member events and tournaments. For many members, these groups are a large part of club life.

**Community and neighborhood groups.** Some residential communities have informal golf groups among neighbors, even where the course is public.

**Informal regular games.** Many golfers simply find two or three others and book a standing tee time.

## Step 1: Get a handicap

Most organized groups use handicaps so players of different abilities can compete fairly. In Arizona, handicaps are managed through **AZ GOLF**, the organization formerly known as the Arizona Golf Association, using the **GHIN** system, which AZ GOLF describes as the largest handicap management service in the world.

Many associations sign you up for a handicap as part of joining. Some include the handicap fee in their dues. Others charge it separately. One Arizona women’s golf association, for example, listed a $45 AZ GOLF handicap fee, and noted that members belonging to more than one association could receive a rebate. Ask each group how it handles handicaps.

If you already have a handicap from another state, ask AZ GOLF or your new group how to transfer or continue it.

## Step 2: Ask at the golf shop

The single best source of information is the golf shop at the courses you play most. Ask:

- What men’s, women’s and mixed groups or associations play here?
- Which days and times do they play?
- What are the annual dues, and what do they include?
- Is a handicap required, and do you handle the sign-up?
- Are new members welcome mid-season?
- Is there a group for beginners or for nine-hole players?

Public facilities with more than one course, such as TPC Scottsdale, Grayhawk, Troon North and Talking Stick, often have more going on. But smaller courses can have the most welcoming groups.

## Step 3: Try before you commit

Ask whether you can play once or twice as a guest before joining. It is the best way to see:

- **The pace of play.** Some groups play quickly, others take their time.
- **The level of competition.** Some groups are serious about their games, others are mostly social.
- **The schedule.** Early tee times, weekday mornings or weekend afternoons.
- **The people.** The right group is the one you look forward to seeing every week.

## What it costs

Group costs usually have three parts:

1. **Annual association or league dues**, which vary widely
2. **A handicap fee**, sometimes included in dues
3. **Green fees** for each round, which depend on the course and season

Green fees in Scottsdale vary sharply by season. Peak-season rates at the most popular public courses can run several hundred dollars a round, while summer rates are often much lower. Ask whether your group has negotiated rates.

## Private clubs and their golf groups

For many residents, regular games are the main reason to join a private club. Clubs commonly organize:

- Men’s, women’s and mixed weekly games
- Member-guest and club championship events
- Social golf, such as evening nines or couples events
- Clinics and practice groups

At larger clubs with several courses, such as Desert Mountain, organized games are one of the easiest ways to meet other members. Desert Mountain’s par-54 No. 7 course was built partly for shorter, more social rounds.

If you are considering a club, ask how its regular games work, how new members are welcomed and how tee times for groups are allocated. Our [guide to joining a Scottsdale private club](/blog/how-to-join-a-private-golf-club-in-scottsdale) covers the process.

## Groups for beginners and returning golfers

If you are new to golf or returning after a break:

- **Look for nine-hole groups.** Many associations and clubs have them.
- **Look for scramble or team formats**, which keep pace up and pressure down.
- **Take lessons first.** Clinics at local courses are a good way to meet other new golfers.
- **Choose forgiving courses.** Our [guide to Scottsdale golf courses for beginners](/blog/best-scottsdale-golf-courses-for-beginners-2026) lists good options.

## Golf groups in residential communities

If you live in a golf community, your neighbors may already have a group. In private-club communities, the club usually runs organized games. In communities built around public golf, such as Grayhawk and Troon North, groups of residents often form informally around the public courses. Ask your HOA, community newsletter or neighbors, and check whether your community hosts golf or social events where you can meet other players.

## Women’s golf groups

Women’s golf associations are common across Arizona and typically run their handicaps through AZ GOLF. Many host weekly play days, nine-hole and eighteen-hole divisions, and member events. Ask the golf shops near you which women’s associations they host and how to join.

## Fitting in with a new group

- **Arrive early** and check in on time.
- **Know the format** before the round starts.
- **Keep up the pace.** Play ready golf, especially in large groups.
- **Post your scores** if the group requires it, so your handicap stays current.
- **Help out.** Volunteering for scorekeeping or events is the fastest way to become part of the group.

## Playing through the seasons

Scottsdale’s golf calendar shapes group life. From fall through spring, many groups are at their largest, as part-year residents return. In summer, groups often shrink, move to very early tee times or play nine holes. Some courses close for a few weeks in the fall to overseed their grass, so groups may move temporarily. Ask your group how it handles summer and overseeding.

## Starting your own group

If you cannot find the right group, start one. It is easier than it sounds.

1. **Find three to seven regular players**, from neighbors, work, your community or people you meet on the course.
2. **Pick a regular day and time.** Consistency is what keeps groups together.
3. **Book a standing tee time** if the course allows it, or take turns booking.
4. **Agree on a simple format**, such as a weekly game with small stakes or a points system.
5. **Use a group message** to confirm who is playing each week.
6. **Rotate courses occasionally** to keep it interesting. Scottsdale has plenty to choose from.

As the group grows, you may want to formalize it as an association, which can make it easier to run handicapped competitions. Ask AZ GOLF how associations are set up.

## Choosing the right group: a quick comparison

- **Public-course association:** affordable, welcoming to new players, set schedule, competitive formats. Best for golfers who play one or two days a week.
- **League:** structured, team-based, sometimes rotating between courses. Best for golfers who like competition and variety.
- **Private club game:** built into club life, often daily, with a membership cost. Best for golfers who play several times a week and want a social club.
- **Informal standing game:** flexible and free beyond green fees. Best for golfers who already know a few regular partners.

## Golf as a way to meet people

For many people who move to Scottsdale, a golf group becomes their first circle of friends. Groups often extend beyond the course to post-round lunches, social events and trips. If meeting people is part of your goal, look for a group with a social side, and say yes to the lunches.

## For part-year residents

If you live in Scottsdale only part of the year, look for groups that welcome seasonal members. Ask whether dues are prorated, whether your handicap can stay active year-round and whether the group connects with groups where you spend the rest of the year.
