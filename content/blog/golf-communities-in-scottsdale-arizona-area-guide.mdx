Scottsdale has one of the deepest collections of golf communities in the country, and they are not interchangeable. Two communities a few miles apart can have completely different golf, different costs and a different daily life. The fastest way to make sense of them is to look at two things together: where a community is, and how its golf actually works.

This guide does exactly that. It is organized by area, from central Scottsdale north to Carefree and east to Rio Verde, and it sorts communities by access type. It does not rank them. For that, see our [2026 Scottsdale golf communities index](/blog/scottsdale-golf-communities-ranked-2026).

*Important: This is general editorial information, not legal, tax or financial advice. Club ownership, fees and rules change. Verify current details with each club, HOA and a licensed real estate professional before making a decision.*

## First, the four ways golf works

Before looking at areas, it helps to know the four basic models you will see.

**Member-owned private clubs.** The members collectively own the club, elect a board and share the costs. Membership usually involves an initiation fee, monthly dues and possible assessments. Public sources describe Desert Mountain Club, Mirabel Golf Club, The Estancia Club, The Country Club at DC Ranch and Tonto Verde Golf Club as member-owned.

**Privately owned private clubs.** A company or individual owns the club. Members pay to use it but do not own it. Invited owns Gainey Ranch Golf Club, Arcis Golf owns Ancala Country Club, and Scottsdale National Golf Club is owned by Bob and Renee Parsons.

**Public and resort golf.** Anyone can play by paying a green fee. Homes nearby carry no club obligation. Grayhawk and Troon North are the best-known examples, and The Boulders combines a resort with an optional club.

**Clubs without homes.** Some of the most prominent clubs have no residential community of their own. Whisper Rock is one. Scottsdale National has built member villas but no conventional neighborhood. Members of both live elsewhere.

Our [equity vs. non-equity guide](/blog/equity-vs-non-equity-golf-memberships-scottsdale-clubs) explains the ownership models in detail.

## Central Scottsdale

Central Scottsdale is home to some of the city’s most established golf neighborhoods. Communities such as **McCormick Ranch** and **Gainey Ranch** date from earlier decades of Scottsdale’s growth and sit close to shopping, dining, medical care and the resort corridor.

**Gainey Ranch** is built around Gainey Ranch Golf Club, which is owned by Invited, the club operator formerly known as ClubCorp. **Ancala** is built around Ancala Country Club, owned by Arcis Golf. Both are examples of private clubs owned by a company rather than by their members.

Who it suits: buyers who want golf close to everyday life, shorter drives to the airport and Old Town, and mature neighborhoods. The trade-off is smaller lots and less of the remote high-desert feel found farther north.

## The DC Ranch, Grayhawk and Pinnacle Peak corridor

Moving north, the middle band of North Scottsdale holds several of the area’s best-known golf communities.

**Grayhawk.** Grayhawk Golf Club opened in 1994 with two public courses: Talon, designed by David Graham and Gary Panks, and Raptor, designed by Tom Fazio. Raptor hosted the PGA TOUR’s Frys.com Open from 2007 to 2009. No membership is required to live in Grayhawk.

**DC Ranch.** A large master-planned community whose club, The Country Club at DC Ranch, is described by public sources as member-owned.

**Silverleaf.** A private enclave within the DC Ranch area, alongside the McDowell Sonoran Preserve, developed by DMB. Silverleaf Club’s Tom Weiskopf course opened in 2002, and the club offers Golf and Clubhouse memberships.

**Troon North.** Near Pinnacle Peak, Troon North is built around Troon North Golf Club, whose two public courses are Monument (1990, Tom Weiskopf and Jay Morrish) and Pinnacle (1995, Tom Weiskopf). There is no mandatory club.

**Desert Highlands.** Developer Lyle Anderson’s private community, with a Jack Nicklaus course that opened in 1984. Public sources describe its club membership as tied to owning property in the community.

Who it suits: buyers who want a strong mix of private and public golf, a wide range of home prices and reasonable drives to North Scottsdale shopping and services.

## Far north Scottsdale

At the city’s northern edge, communities get larger, higher and more private.

**Desert Mountain.** About 8,300 acres in the 85262 ZIP code, bordering Carefree and the Tonto National Forest. Desert Mountain Club has been member-owned since 2011 and has six Jack Nicklaus Signature courses plus No. 7, a par-54 course that opened in 2019.

**Estancia.** A smaller private community with a member-owned club built around a single Tom Fazio course.

**Mirabel.** A private community with a member-owned club and a Tom Fazio course. Public sources say Mirabel no longer requires property ownership for membership.

**Legend Trail.** A community built around a course designed by Arthur Jack Snyder and Forrest Richardson, with Rees Jones brought in during construction.

**Terravita.** A golf community in the far north with its own club.

**Whisper Rock and Scottsdale National.** Two of the area’s most prominent private clubs are also in the far north. Neither has a residential community of its own, so members live in surrounding communities.

Who it suits: buyers who want private-club golf, larger lots, higher elevation and desert views, and who are comfortable with longer drives to shopping, services and the airport.

## Carefree and Cave Creek

Just beyond Scottsdale’s northern edge, Carefree and Cave Creek add a small-town character.

**The Boulders.** Spanning Scottsdale and Carefree, The Boulders is built around Boulders Resort and Spa Scottsdale, Curio Collection by Hilton, and two Jay Morrish courses. The Boulders Club offers golf and social memberships, but membership is not required to own a home.

**Desert Forest Golf Club.** In Carefree, designed by Robert “Red” Lawrence and opened in 1962, Desert Forest is often described as one of the first desert golf courses in the Southwest.

Carefree itself was established as a planned community in 1955. Cave Creek, slightly west, has a western character and a lively strip of restaurants.

Who it suits: buyers who want a resort or small-town feel and a quieter setting, with Scottsdale amenities a drive away.

## Rio Verde and the east

East of Scottsdale, **Tonto Verde** in Rio Verde is a golf community whose club public sources describe as member-owned. The area is quieter and more remote than most Scottsdale neighborhoods.

Who it suits: buyers who prioritize quiet and value, and who are comfortable with long drives to services.

## What each model tends to cost

The access type is the biggest driver of what a golf community costs you each year, often more than the price of the home.

**Member-owned and privately owned private clubs** usually charge an initiation fee plus monthly dues, and may add food minimums and assessments. Published 2026 estimates for Scottsdale-area private clubs put initiation at roughly $35,000 to $350,000 or more and monthly dues at roughly $800 to $3,500, with the most exclusive clubs estimated above that range. These are third-party estimates, so verify them with each club.

**Public and resort golf communities** carry no club bill. You pay green fees when you play, plus your HOA assessments. For someone who plays occasionally, this is usually far cheaper.

**Clubs without homes** mean two separate costs: the club and your housing elsewhere. If your home is in a private-club community too, you may end up paying for two clubs.

HOA assessments apply in every model and are separate from club costs. Our [2026 club membership cost guide](/scottsdale-club-membership-costs-2026) compares published ranges across Scottsdale clubs.

## A note for part-year owners

Many Scottsdale golf buyers live here only part of the year. For them, a few points matter more:

- **Full-year dues.** Private clubs usually charge dues year-round, whether or not you are in town. Ask whether seasonal or non-resident categories exist.
- **Lock-and-leave homes.** Attached and patio homes in many communities let the HOA handle more of the exterior while you are away.
- **Rental rules.** If you plan to rent while you are gone, check the HOA rules. Some communities restrict short-term rentals.
- **Summer care.** Plan for someone to check the home, pool and air conditioning during the hottest months.

## How to choose

1. **Decide how you want golf to work.** Do you want a private club built into your community, public golf nearby with no club bill, or a club elsewhere with a home wherever you like?
2. **Count your drives.** Time the drive from each community to the places you go every week: groceries, medical care, the airport, work or school.
3. **Price the full cost.** Add HOA assessments, club initiation and dues where they apply, and property taxes and insurance. Private-club communities can carry much higher annual costs.
4. **Think about the season.** Part-year owners often prefer lock-and-leave homes and communities without full-year club dues.
5. **Visit in summer and in season.** The same community can feel very different in July and in February.
6. **Read the documents.** For any home you consider, get the HOA and club documents and review them with a licensed professional.
