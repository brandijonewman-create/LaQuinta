For many Scottsdale golf households, the kitchen gets busy. Friends come to town for golf trips, the club calendar fills up, and part-year residents want to make the most of every week they are here. Hiring a private or personal chef is one way households handle it, from a single special dinner to help all season long.

This guide explains the main ways golf households use chefs, what published sources say it costs and how to hire well. It does not recommend specific chefs. Availability changes, and the right chef depends on your household.

*Important: This is general editorial information, not legal, tax, employment or financial advice. Prices and policies change. Confirm every detail in writing with the chef, and talk to your insurer and a licensed tax or employment professional before setting up any ongoing arrangement.*

## Three ways golf households use chefs

**1. The one-night dinner party.** A chef plans a menu, shops, cooks in your kitchen and often serves and cleans up. It is popular for golf trips with friends, anniversaries, club events hosted at home and holiday gatherings.

**2. Weekly meal prep.** A chef cooks a set of meals once or twice a week, stores them in your refrigerator and leaves reheating instructions. It suits busy households, golfers training seriously and anyone with dietary goals.

**3. Seasonal or ongoing arrangements.** Some part-year residents hire a chef for the months they are in Scottsdale, sometimes several days a week. This is the most involved option and raises the most questions about employment and insurance.

## What published sources say it costs

Prices depend on the menu, number of courses, number of guests, ingredients and service. Here is what published sources show.

**One-time dinners in the Phoenix and Scottsdale area**

- A booking platform’s Phoenix guidance puts entry-level dinners at about $59 per person, with higher-end menus over $100.
- Individual platform listings in the area run roughly $99 to $139 per person for multi-course dinners.
- Another platform lists two-hour private chef dinners starting at about $130 per person.
- A Scottsdale caterer lists menus from about $100 to $175 per person.

**National guidance**

- Roughly $50 to $130 per person for dinners
- A one-time dinner-party fee of roughly $300 to $1,600 for the chef’s services, in some pricing models
- Roughly $200 to $750 a week for weekly meal prep, usually plus groceries

These are listing prices and general estimates, not quotes. Wine pairings, premium ingredients, extra staff, rentals and travel can add significantly. Always get an itemized written quote.

## What the price should include

Ask each chef to spell out:

- **Menu planning and consultation**
- **Groceries**, and whether they are included or billed separately at cost
- **Cooking and service**, and how many staff will attend
- **Cleanup**, including dishes and kitchen
- **Rentals**, such as plates, glassware or tables for larger groups
- **Travel**, especially to far-north communities
- **Gratuity**, and whether it is included
- **Cancellation terms** and deposits

## A worked example

Here is how a dinner for eight might add up, using the published Phoenix and Scottsdale listing ranges above. Your quote will differ.

- **Per-person menu:** at roughly $100 to $175 per person, eight guests come to about $800 to $1,400
- **Wine:** your own bottles, or a pairing priced separately
- **Extra staff:** a server or bartender for a larger group, often billed separately
- **Rentals:** usually unnecessary for eight, but common for larger parties
- **Gratuity:** ask whether it is included, and budget for it if not

For a weekly meal-prep arrangement at the national guidance of roughly $200 to $750 a week plus groceries, a twelve-week winter season would come to roughly $2,400 to $9,000 for the chef’s service alone.

## Chef, caterer or restaurant?

**A private chef** cooks in your kitchen, often for smaller groups, with a custom menu and a personal feel.

**A caterer** usually prepares food elsewhere and delivers or serves it, and is often the better fit for large parties, buffets or events with many guests.

**A restaurant private dining room** takes cooking and cleanup out of your home entirely, which suits some groups better, especially in peak season.

For a golf group of six to twelve who want to relax at home after a round, a private chef is often the sweet spot.

## Menu ideas for golf nights

- **After a long round:** grilled meats or fish, seasonal salads and simple desserts, served on the patio
- **Member-guest celebration:** a multi-course dinner with wine pairings
- **Golf trip with friends:** family-style platters that are easy to share
- **Training season:** high-protein meal prep with clear labels and reheating notes

## How to hire well

**Step 1: Define the job.** One dinner, weekly meals or a season? How many people, how often and what kinds of food?

**Step 2: Gather candidates.** Ask friends, neighbors and your club for recommendations, and look at booking platforms if you want to compare menus and reviews.

**Step 3: Review menus and experience.** Ask for sample menus and about the chef’s background, including restaurant or private experience.

**Step 4: Ask about food safety and allergies.** Ask about food safety training and how the chef handles allergies and dietary restrictions. Be specific about any allergies in your household or among guests.

**Step 5: Check insurance.** Ask whether the chef carries liability insurance, and ask your own insurer how a chef working in your home affects your coverage.

**Step 6: Check references.** Talk to two or three recent clients.

**Step 7: Try one meal.** Before a big event or an ongoing arrangement, book a smaller dinner first.

**Step 8: Put it in writing.** Menu, price, what is included, timing, cancellation and payment.

## Planning for your kitchen

Chefs work best in a kitchen that is ready for them.

- **Walk through the kitchen together** before the event. Show them the equipment, storage and any quirks.
- **Clear counter and refrigerator space.**
- **Confirm the appliances work,** especially ovens and grills.
- **Plan outdoor cooking** if the chef will use a grill or outdoor kitchen, and check that it is clean and fueled.

For homes in gated communities, arrange gate access for the chef and any staff ahead of time.

## Golf-household use cases

**The golf trip with friends.** A chef-cooked dinner at home after a long day on the course is often more relaxed than a restaurant for a large group.

**The post-tournament dinner.** Hosting your foursome or team after a member-guest or club event.

**Training and nutrition.** Golfers working on fitness sometimes use meal prep to stick to a plan. If you have specific health goals, talk to a licensed medical or nutrition professional as well.

**Part-year residents.** Arriving for the season to a stocked refrigerator and a few weeks of meals can make the transition easier.

## Seasonal and ongoing arrangements

If you want a chef several days a week for a season, the arrangement starts to look more like employment. That raises questions about:

- **Whether the chef is an independent contractor or a household employee**
- **Payroll taxes and reporting**
- **Insurance**, including liability and workers’ compensation
- **Written agreements** covering hours, duties, pay and termination

These rules can be complex. Talk to a licensed tax or employment professional and your insurer before you begin.

## Etiquette and logistics on the night

- **Agree on arrival and service times** so the chef can plan around your tee time or event.
- **Share guest details early,** including allergies, preferences and any guests arriving late.
- **Set up the bar yourself** or ask whether the chef’s team will handle it.
- **Decide on cleanup** before the night, so there are no surprises.
- **Tip if gratuity is not included,** and say thank you. Good chefs book up quickly in peak season, and a good relationship helps.

## Booking in peak season

Fall through spring is Scottsdale’s busiest season, and popular chefs can book up weeks ahead, especially around holidays and major golf events. Book early for important dinners, and have a backup plan.

## Red flags

- No written quote, or a quote that does not say what is included
- Reluctance to discuss food safety or allergies
- No references
- Pressure to pay large deposits far in advance without clear cancellation terms
- No insurance, for larger or ongoing work

## Questions to ask a private chef

1. What kinds of events and households do you usually cook for?
2. Can I see sample menus?
3. What food safety training do you have?
4. How do you handle allergies and dietary restrictions?
5. Do you carry liability insurance?
6. What exactly does your quote include?
7. Do you provide service staff and cleanup?
8. What are your deposit and cancellation terms?
9. Can you provide references from recent clients?
