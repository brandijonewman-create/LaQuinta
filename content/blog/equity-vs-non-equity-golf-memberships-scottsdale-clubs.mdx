Our [equity vs. non-equity primer](/blog/scottsdale-golf-club-equity-vs-non-equity) explains the two membership models in general terms. This article goes one step further: it looks at how specific clubs in and around Scottsdale are actually owned, because the owner of the club is what decides what your initiation fee really buys.

Everything below comes from public sources: club websites, operator websites and industry reporting. Where sources disagree, we say so. Cost figures are given as ranges from published estimates, not from club fee schedules, because most private clubs in the area do not publish their prices.

*Important: This is general editorial information, not legal, tax or financial advice. Membership terms, fees and ownership change. Verify every figure and term with the club directly and with a licensed real estate, legal or financial professional before you make a decision.*

## The one question that matters most: who owns the club?

Every private club has an owner. That owner falls into one of two broad groups.

**Equity (member-owned) clubs.** The members collectively own the club, usually through a nonprofit or member corporation. Members elect a board, and the board sets dues, approves capital projects and decides on assessments. Your initiation typically buys you a share of that ownership, and the club's documents say what happens to it when you leave.

**Non-equity clubs.** A company, a developer or an individual owns the club. You pay an initiation fee and dues for the right to use it, but you do not own any part of it. The owner makes the big decisions, including whether to invest in the course, raise dues or sell the club.

Neither model is automatically better. A member-owned club gives members control, but members also carry the costs, including assessments for new clubhouses or course renovations. An owner-operated club can bring outside capital and professional management, but members have less say over its future.

## Member-owned clubs in the Scottsdale area

### Desert Mountain Club

Desert Mountain is the best-documented example of members taking over a club. In early 2011, members completed a deal with owner Crescent Real Estate Holdings to buy the club's six golf courses, related facilities and about 500 acres of developable land for $73.5 million, as reported by Golf Course Industry. The club's own timeline lists 2011 as the year ownership transferred to the members.

Today Desert Mountain describes itself as offering the largest collection of private Jack Nicklaus Signature golf courses, plus a par-54 course called Seven. For a buyer, the practical point is that Desert Mountain's members, through their elected board, now make the decisions a developer used to make.

### Mirabel Golf Club

According to Arizona Foothills Magazine, Mirabel has been member-owned since 2009. Industry coverage describes it as member-owned and debt-free, and notes that it is member-owned but not member-run: the board sets strategy and the professional staff runs day-to-day operations.

Mirabel is also a useful example of how resale can work at an equity club. Sources describe its membership pricing as market-based, meaning members can set the price at which they sell their own membership. Mirabel has also lowered its membership caps over time. Property ownership in the community is no longer required to join.

### The Estancia Club

The Estancia Club, at the base of Pinnacle Peak, describes itself as a private club owned and operated by its members. Play is limited to members and their invited guests, and guests may play only when hosted by a member. The course was designed by Tom Fazio and opened in 1995.

### The Country Club at DC Ranch

The Country Club at DC Ranch was originally owned by its developer, DMB Associates. DMB later handed ownership to the members after a transition led by a member turnover committee, and the club now describes itself as member-owned. Some listings describe an equity golf membership alongside a non-equity sport and social category. That combination is common: one club can offer both an equity category and a non-equity category, with different rights attached to each.

### Tonto Verde Golf Club

Out in Rio Verde, Tonto Verde Golf Club describes itself on its own website as a private club owned by its members. It has two 18-hole courses, the Peaks and the Ranch, and the club says it accepts non-resident memberships. Some directories report that the club sells limited public tee times, so if access matters to you, ask the club how often non-members play.

## Clubs owned by a company or an individual

### Gainey Ranch Golf Club and Anthem Golf & Country Club (Invited)

Invited, the company formerly known as ClubCorp, owns and operates both Gainey Ranch Golf Club in Scottsdale and Anthem Golf & Country Club in Anthem. At Anthem, golf membership is open to Anthem property owners and Anthem Parkside residents, subject to application, approval and an initiation fee, and the club lists several categories, including full golf, classic golf, tennis and social.

When a large operator owns a club, members often gain access to a wider network of clubs. The trade-off is that the operator, not the members, decides the club's long-term direction.

### Ancala Country Club and Tatum Ranch Golf Club (Arcis Golf)

Arcis Golf, whose parent is the private equity firm Arcis Equity Partners, owns Ancala Country Club in Scottsdale and Tatum Ranch Golf Club in Cave Creek. Arcis markets both as full-service private clubs. Arcis also sells a regional subscription product that gives golfers access to its courses in a region for a monthly fee with no initiation fee, which shows how differently an operator can package golf access compared with a member-owned club.

We did not find a source that labels Ancala or Tatum Ranch memberships as "non-equity" in those exact words. Ownership by Arcis means members do not own the club, but the specific terms of each membership are set in its own documents, so read them.

### Scottsdale National Golf Club

Scottsdale National is owned by an entity formed by Bob Parsons, the founder of GoDaddy and PXG. The club describes itself as invitation-only, with three ways to play: as a member, as a member's guest, or through its Xperience program, which is by application and invitation. Club & Resort Business has reported that when Parsons changed club policies, he offered members who disagreed a full refund of their membership if they left by a set deadline. That is a good illustration of the non-equity model: the owner sets the terms, and members decide whether to stay.

## A third pattern: membership tied to owning a home

Some clubs link membership to property ownership, whatever their ownership model. Published descriptions of Desert Highlands say membership is tied to owning property in the community, and that buyers pay a non-refundable transfer fee to the club when they purchase. Anthem Golf & Country Club limits golf membership to Anthem property owners and Anthem Parkside residents. Mirabel used to require property ownership but no longer does.

If membership comes with the house, the cost of the club is effectively part of the cost of the home. Ask how the transfer fee works, who pays it, and what happens to the membership when you sell.

## What it costs: published ranges for 2026

Most Scottsdale private clubs do not publish their fees, and the figures below are estimates from secondary sources, not quotes from any club. Use them to set expectations, then get real numbers from the club.

- **Initiation fees:** roughly $35,000 to $350,000, according to one regional guide, with many well-regarded clubs below the top tier in roughly the $50,000 to $150,000 range. Individual reports for the most exclusive clubs run higher.
- **Monthly dues:** roughly $800 to $2,500, depending on the club and the category. The same guide puts the all-in monthly cost for an active golf member at roughly $1,500 to $3,500.
- **Extras:** food and beverage minimums, assessments and other fees can add roughly $10,000 to $30,000 a year on top of dues, according to a 2026 industry article.

*These are published estimates, not verified club prices. Fees change often and vary by membership category. Confirm current figures with the club and review them with a licensed professional before you commit.*

## Four questions to ask any club before you join

**1. Who owns the club today?** Ask for the name of the owning entity. If it is a member corporation, ask how the board is elected. If it is a company or individual, ask whether the owner has any plans to sell.

**2. What happens to my initiation when I leave?** At an equity club, ask whether any part of the initiation comes back, when, and whether you must wait for a new member to buy in. At a non-equity club, ask whether any of the initiation is refundable at all. Mirabel's market-based model and Scottsdale National's one-time refund offer show how different the answers can be.

**3. How are assessments decided?** At a member-owned club, members may vote on major capital projects, but once approved, every member typically pays. At an owner-operated club, the owner usually funds improvements but may raise dues. Ask for the history of assessments and dues increases over recent years.

**4. Is membership tied to my home?** If it is, the transfer fee and membership rules belong in your home-buying math, and you should understand them before you make an offer.

## How to use this information

Start with how you will actually use the club. If you plan to stay for many years and want a say in how the club is run, a member-owned club may suit you, as long as you are comfortable sharing the cost of future projects. If you want flexibility, a lower entry cost or access to a wider network, an owner-operated club may suit you better.

Then read the documents. The membership agreement, bylaws and any resale or refund policy decide your rights, not the sales conversation. A licensed real estate professional who knows Scottsdale clubs, along with your attorney and tax advisor, can help you read them in the context of your home purchase.
