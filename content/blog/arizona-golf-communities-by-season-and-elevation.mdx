Most places have one golf season. Arizona has two, separated by elevation. The low desert around Phoenix and Tucson is at its best from fall through spring, while the high country around Prescott, Sedona and Flagstaff comes into its own in summer. For a golfer choosing where to live, that makes elevation one of the most important factors of all.

This guide looks at Arizona golf communities through the lens of season and elevation. For a region-by-region tour of the state’s golf communities, see our [statewide guide to Arizona golf communities](/blog/arizona-golf-communities-statewide-guide-2026).

*Important: This is general editorial information, not legal, tax or financial advice. Elevation figures vary by source and by where they are measured. Community details, rules and costs change. Verify everything with each community and work with a licensed real estate, tax or financial professional before making a decision.*

## Arizona’s elevation bands

Published elevation figures vary depending on whether they measure a city center, an airport or an average, but the general picture is clear. Approximate figures:

- **Phoenix:** about 1,100 feet
- **Tucson:** about 2,400 feet
- **Cave Creek:** about 2,200 feet
- **Desert Mountain (far north Scottsdale):** roughly 2,500 to 4,500 feet, depending on location within the community
- **Sedona:** about 4,350 feet
- **Prescott:** about 5,400 feet
- **Flagstaff:** about 7,000 feet

As a rough rule, the higher you go, the cooler the summers and the colder the winters. That determines when golf is comfortable.

## The low desert: winter golf

**Where:** Phoenix, Scottsdale, the surrounding suburbs and Tucson.

**Best season:** fall through spring. Winter days are generally mild and sunny, which is why the low desert draws so many winter residents and golf travelers.

**Summer:** very hot. Golf is still possible, but it moves to very early tee times, and many part-year residents leave.

**Course conditions:** many low-desert courses overseed their grass in the fall, which can mean temporary closures or changing conditions. Ask each club or course about its schedule.

**Example communities:**

- Scottsdale’s golf communities, from Gainey Ranch and McCormick Ranch in the center to Grayhawk, Troon North and the far north
- Superstition Mountain in Gold Canyon, with two Nicklaus family courses
- Tucson-area communities such as Dove Mountain, SaddleBrooke and Sun City Oro Valley

## The middle band: far north Scottsdale, Tucson and Sedona

Some places sit between the low desert and the high country, with a longer comfortable season than either extreme.

**Far north Scottsdale.** Communities such as Desert Mountain sit well above the Valley floor. Published figures for Desert Mountain range from roughly 2,500 to 4,500 feet. That usually means slightly milder temperatures than central Phoenix, though summer is still hot. Our [guide to Scottsdale’s northernmost communities](/blog/far-north-scottsdale-communities-guide) covers the area.

**Tucson.** At about 2,400 feet, Tucson sits a little higher than Phoenix, with a similar winter golf season.

**Sedona.** At about 4,350 feet, Sedona has milder summers than the low desert and milder winters than Flagstaff. Seven Canyons Golf Club, designed by Tom Weiskopf and opened in 2003, sits among the red rocks in the Coconino National Forest.

## The high country: summer golf

**Where:** Prescott and Flagstaff, and other mountain towns.

**Best season:** late spring through early fall. Summer temperatures are far below the desert, which makes the high country a natural summer escape for desert residents.

**Winter:** colder, with snow possible, especially in Flagstaff. Golf seasons are shorter, and some courses close or limit play in winter.

**Example communities:**

- **Talking Rock** in Prescott, a private club with a Jay Morrish course designed with walkability in mind
- **Pine Canyon** in Flagstaff, a private mountain community with a Jay Morrish course that opened in 2004

## Choosing by how you live

**If you live in Arizona year-round,** decide which season matters most. A low-desert community gives you the best winter golf and the most amenities, with hot summers. A high-country community gives you comfortable summers and a shorter winter season. A middle-band location such as far north Scottsdale or Sedona can stretch the comfortable season in both directions.

**If you are a winter resident,** the low desert is the natural choice. Look at lock-and-leave homes and club categories that suit part-year members.

**If you are a summer resident,** look north to Prescott, Sedona or Flagstaff.

**If you want to golf all year,** consider two homes, or one home plus regular trips.

## The two-home golf year

Arizona makes a two-home golf life possible within a few hours’ drive. A common pattern is a low-desert home for winter and a high-country home for summer. Before you commit, think through:

- **Two sets of carrying costs:** HOA assessments, property taxes, insurance and utilities for both homes
- **Two club decisions:** you may want a membership in one place, both or neither. Private clubs usually charge dues year-round.
- **Reciprocal play:** some private clubs arrange access to other clubs, which can reduce the need for two memberships. Ask each club.
- **Lock-and-leave fit:** whichever home sits empty needs care. Attached homes where the association handles more of the exterior are easier to leave.
- **Rental rules:** if you plan to rent either home while away, check the rules. Some communities restrict short-term rentals.
- **Tax and residency questions:** a licensed tax professional can explain how owning two homes affects your situation.

## Golf at elevation

Golfers who move between the desert and the high country often notice the ball traveling farther in the thinner air at higher elevations. Courses designed for high elevations take this into account, but it can take a few rounds to adjust your club selection when you switch between homes.

## How the landscape changes with elevation

Elevation changes not only the temperature but the look and feel of the golf.

- **Low desert:** saguaros, palo verde and creosote, with courses routed through washes and rock. This is the classic Sonoran Desert golf of Scottsdale and Tucson.
- **Middle band:** more boulders and higher ridgelines in places such as far north Scottsdale, and red rock around Sedona.
- **High country:** pine forest. Flagstaff sits next to what is described as the largest contiguous ponderosa pine forest in North America, which gives its golf a mountain character far removed from the desert.

For many golfers, that variety is the real appeal of Arizona. Within a few hours’ drive, you can play desert, red rock and pine forest golf.

## Monsoon season

Across much of Arizona, the summer monsoon brings afternoon storms, wind, dust and lightning, typically in mid to late summer. For golfers, that means watching the forecast, starting early, knowing where to shelter and following each course’s lightning policy. Storms can also affect course conditions for a day or two afterward.

## Staying comfortable at any elevation

- **In the desert,** play early in summer, carry plenty of water and protect yourself from the sun year-round.
- **In the high country,** the sun is still strong, and the thinner air can leave visitors short of breath at first. Give yourself a few days to adjust.
- **Moving between homes,** expect your distances and energy levels to change for the first few rounds.

## A season-by-season calendar

**Fall:** the low desert reopens for the season, though some courses close briefly to overseed. High-country courses wind down as temperatures fall.

**Winter:** peak golf in the low desert, with the highest demand and prices. High-country golf is limited, especially in Flagstaff.

**Spring:** excellent golf in the low desert and the middle band. The high country begins to open up.

**Summer:** the high country’s peak. In the low desert, golf moves to dawn, rates fall and many part-year residents leave.

## Budgeting by season

Season affects what golf costs, not just when it is comfortable. In the low desert, public-course green fees are generally highest in winter and spring and lowest in summer. One published example: We-Ko-Pa’s 2026 rates for 36 holes ranged from about $179 in summer to about $579 in peak season. Private clubs usually charge dues year-round regardless of season, so part-year members should weigh full-year dues against the months they will actually play.

## Questions to ask any Arizona golf community

1. What are the best and worst months for golf here?
2. Does the course close for overseeding or winter, and when?
3. What are the HOA assessments, and what do they cover while I am away?
4. Is club membership required, optional or tied to owning property?
5. Does the club offer seasonal, non-resident or reciprocal options?
6. What are the rental rules?
7. How far is the nearest airport, hospital and major shopping?

Get the answers in writing and review them with a licensed real estate professional.

## Where Scottsdale fits

Scottsdale is Arizona’s deepest concentration of golf communities and private clubs, and its far north reaches into the middle elevation band. For many golfers, it is the winter anchor of an Arizona golf life, paired with summer golf in the high country, or with a summer home elsewhere entirely.
