If you are reading this you have likely already done the obvious homework. You know Desert Mountain has six Jack Nicklaus courses. You know it sits at the northern edge of Scottsdale, Arizona. You know home prices start well above $1M. What you probably do not yet know, and what this guide exists to make explicit, is how the neighborhoods inside Desert Mountain differ from one another, how club membership actually works, and which buyers the community suits and which it does not.

Desert Mountain is not for everyone. It is actively wrong for several types of buyers, and we will say so plainly in the second half of this article. But for the right buyer it is one of the most complete golf-community propositions in the country. Let us walk through what that means in 2026.

*Important: This is general editorial information, not legal, tax or financial advice. Prices, fees and rules change. Verify every figure with the club, the HOA and a licensed real estate, legal or financial professional before you make a decision.*

## What Desert Mountain actually is

Desert Mountain covers about 8,300 acres of high Sonoran Desert at the northern edge of Scottsdale, roughly 30 miles from Phoenix Sky Harbor International Airport. Inside the gates sit six Jack Nicklaus Signature golf courses, a par-54 course called No. 7, and a club campus that, by the club’s own count, includes seven clubhouses, ten restaurants and grills, a racquet complex with nine tennis courts and eight pickleball courts, and more than 25 miles of private hiking trails.

The community began in 1986 under developer Lyle Anderson, who also created Desert Highlands in Scottsdale, Las Campanas in Santa Fe and Loch Lomond Golf Club in Scotland. His site-planning approach, letting the terrain drive the routing and the home sites, is a large part of why Desert Mountain still does not look like a 1980s development four decades on.

In 2011, members bought the club’s six golf courses, related facilities and about 500 acres of developable land from Crescent Real Estate Holdings for a reported $73.5 million. Desert Mountain Club has been member-owned since. The homeowners association and the club are separate organizations, which matters more than it sounds (we cover it below and in our [guide to living in Desert Mountain](/blog/living-in-desert-mountain-hoa-rules-costs-2026)).

## The villages, and how they differ

Desert Mountain is not one neighborhood. It is a collection of separately named subdivisions, usually called villages (one published profile counted 32), connected by a private road network. Each has its own home type, lot sizes, price band and village association. Buyers who treat them as interchangeable tend to end up in the wrong one.

The useful way to sort them is by home type:

- **Attached and patio homes.** Smaller footprints, shared walls or zero-lot-line designs, and village associations that typically handle more of the exterior upkeep. These tend to be the entry point to the community and the most practical choice for part-year owners.
- **Detached homes on smaller desert lots.** The middle of the market, with more privacy than attached product but less upkeep than a large custom lot.
- **Custom estates.** Larger home sites, often in the higher-elevation areas with the longest views. Apache Peak is the best-known example and holds much of the community’s top-end inventory.
- **Newer enclaves.** Recent product such as Seven Desert Mountain, planned for up to about 190 homes, and newer builder communities such as the Villas at Desert Mountain bring contemporary floor plans into a community that is otherwise largely resale.

A serious buyer should expect to compare three to five villages before narrowing. Two homes at the same price in different villages can carry very different assessments, upkeep obligations and neighborhood character, so ask for the village documents on every home you shortlist.

## The seven golf courses

Desert Mountain describes its six Jack Nicklaus Signature courses as the largest private collection of Nicklaus Signature designs anywhere: Renegade, Cochise, Geronimo, Apache, Chiricahua and Outlaw. The seventh, No. 7 at Desert Mountain, is a par-54 course of about 3,100 yards designed by club members Bill Brownlee and Wendell Pickett. It opened in 2019 and has become one of the most social pieces of golf at the club.

Course personality matters when you live here. Cochise carries the most tournament history: it hosted The Tradition, a senior major, in the 1990s, and the PGA TOUR Champions’ Charles Schwab Cup Championship in 2012 and from 2014 through 2016. Members tend to describe Geronimo as one of the friendlier member layouts, Chiricahua as having some of the most dramatic elevation, and Outlaw as the most modern-feeling of the six. You will develop your own favorites quickly, and you will hear about everyone else’s at dinner.

## The membership decision: the single biggest one

Owning a home inside Desert Mountain does not come with golf access. You apply to the club separately and choose a category. The club currently lists three: **Full Golf**, which includes the six Nicklaus courses; **Seven Golf**, built around No. 7; and **Lifestyle**, for dining, fitness, racquet sports and social access without full golf. Wait lists can apply, and memberships for homes in Seven Desert Mountain have been reserved for approved buyers there.

The club does not publish a current fee schedule. Its 2021 FAQ listed initiation fees of $70,000 for Full Golf, $33,750 for Seven Golf and $22,250 for Lifestyle, and said fees could change. Third-party sites now cite much higher figures for some categories, but they disagree with each other. Get a current quote from the membership office before you remove any contingency, and see our cost guide for published ranges across Scottsdale clubs.

## HOA structure

Desert Mountain has a Master Association plus village associations. According to community materials, master assessments cover administration of the master and village associations and maintenance and reserves for Desert Mountain Parkway, Desert Hills Drive and the common areas, and they have been billed twice a year, due January 1 and July 1. Village associations set their own assessments, and what they cover varies.

The club is a separate organization with its own board and its own budget. That means your HOA costs and your club costs are two different lines in your budget, and a change at one does not automatically affect the other. Our [guide to living in Desert Mountain](/blog/living-in-desert-mountain-hoa-rules-costs-2026) has a full list of the costs to collect during escrow and where to get each number.

## Who tends to buy in Desert Mountain

The traditional Desert Mountain owner was a retiree from the Midwest buying a winter home. That buyer still exists. In our conversations with buyers and agents, we also hear regularly from:

- Late-career professionals relocating from higher-tax states who want a full-time Arizona residence
- Families with significant wealth looking for a second home in a private golf setting
- Empty-nesters treating the move as a permanent lifestyle change rather than a seasonal one

The common thread is preparation. Desert Mountain buyers usually arrive having compared several communities, and sellers price with that in mind.

## Who should not buy here

Three buyer types are more likely to regret a Desert Mountain purchase.

1. **Walk-to-everything buyers.** The community is intentionally removed from shopping and dining, and most errands mean a drive. If you want to walk out your door for coffee or dinner, look at Old Town Scottsdale, Kierland or Gainey Ranch instead.
2. **Occasional golfers who don’t want a big club bill.** If you will play a handful of times a year, a Lifestyle category, or no membership at all, may be more honest than Full Golf. The community is built around the assumption that the club is central to your life.
3. **Families who need a short school commute.** Listings for Desert Mountain homes typically name Cave Creek Unified schools, and private-school commutes from this far north are long. Families with school-age children should time the drive and confirm school assignment for the address. Communities such as DC Ranch and Grayhawk are closer to more school options.

## Appreciation and resale

We have not found published, verified data comparing Desert Mountain’s appreciation or days-on-market with the wider Scottsdale luxury market, so we will not make that claim. What is true is that results vary by price band and village: estate inventory and smaller attached homes draw different buyer pools and move on different timelines. Ask your agent for recent ARMLS sales and days-on-market for the specific village and price band you are considering.

## What buying here looks like in practice

Many Desert Mountain purchases are cash, or are financed through private banking relationships that lend against the buyer’s broader balance sheet. If you plan to finance, talk to a lender who regularly handles high-balance loans in north Scottsdale before you make an offer.

During escrow, collect the master and village HOA disclosures, recent board minutes, the current rental rules and the design guidelines for the village. Start the club membership conversation the same week you sign the purchase contract. There is no advantage to waiting until closing, and wait lists can apply.

## How Desert Mountain compares to its peers

The communities most often considered alongside Desert Mountain are Silverleaf (smaller, very private, with strict architectural standards), Estancia (a small member-owned club around a single Tom Fazio course) and Mirabel (a single Tom Fazio course on a smaller scale with a tighter social culture). Each has a case. None has six Nicklaus courses behind one gate.

## The honest framing on size

Desert Mountain is much larger than the other top-tier private communities in Scottsdale. That is a feature for some buyers and a drawback for others.

The feature: scale gives you options. Seven courses mean a member can play a different layout most days without leaving the gate. Multiple clubhouses mean you are not dependent on one dining room. More inventory gives buyers more homes to choose from.

The drawback: scale spreads out the social life. A small club like Estancia has a tight-knit member culture, and a much larger roster cannot feel the same way. Households that want to know every neighbor may find Estancia a better fit. Households that value options and a lower social density will likely prefer Desert Mountain.

## The honest closing

Desert Mountain is not the right answer for a buyer who wants walkability, a short school commute or a low club bill. It is a strong answer for a buyer who plays golf seriously, wants a community built around making that easy, values architectural restraint, and plans to stay long enough for initiation and transaction costs to make sense.

If you fit that profile, our advice is simple: tour at least three villages on different days, have dinner at the club before you decide, and talk to two current members in the village you are considering. Desert Mountain rewards buyers who arrive with their eyes open.
