A significant share of Scottsdale luxury second-home buyers will eventually want to flip the residency designation south. Many will not: they will keep the primary residence elsewhere and maintain Scottsdale as a winter, weekend, or family-vacation home. Both paths have tax implications that benefit from upfront planning. The cost of getting it wrong is large enough that the cost of an hour with a CPA before transacting is one of the highest-leverage spends in the entire process.

This guide is editorial and informational, not tax advice. Every household\u2019s situation differs, and the rules below are simplified for clarity. Always verify with a licensed CPA in both your origin state and Arizona.

## Property tax: the headline favorable differential

Maricopa County\u2019s property tax structure is materially favorable relative to high-tax origin states. The effective annual property tax bill on a $3M Scottsdale home is generally well below the equivalent bill in New Jersey, Illinois, Texas, or California for a similarly-valued home. The structure is complex: it is set by overlapping taxing jurisdictions (county, city, school district, community college district, special districts) and applied as a millage rate against an assessed value that is reset on improvements, but the net effect for most Scottsdale residential parcels has been a meaningful annual saving for relocating households.

The two things to verify during diligence are the historical property tax bills for the specific home (the title work will provide this), and whether any pending bond elections or assessed-value adjustments could move the rate within your expected hold period. Recent improvements that triggered an assessed-value reset can produce a higher applied tax than the historical bill suggests for a recent buyer.

One note on the Arizona system that catches new owners off-guard: the assessed value is not the market value. Arizona uses a "limited property value" formula that can deviate meaningfully from a comparable sales-based market valuation. This is generally favorable to the homeowner, it dampens the year-to-year volatility of the tax bill, but it produces a bill that does not correspond simply to a percentage of what you paid.

## State income tax: the second-order question

Arizona\u2019s top marginal individual income tax rate is among the lowest in the country, materially below California, New York, Illinois, New Jersey, and Massachusetts. Arizona has no state-level estate tax.

For the second-home buyer who is not changing residency, this does not directly affect taxation: income tax follows residency, not property ownership. You can own a Scottsdale home and continue to be taxed by your origin state on your worldwide income while you remain a resident of that state.

The planning question becomes interesting when the household considers flipping residency. For a high-income or high-net-worth household leaving California, New York, or Massachusetts, the long-run savings of an Arizona domicile can be substantial. The mechanics of executing a clean domicile change matter. Several origin states are aggressive about asserting continued residency on departing taxpayers, particularly when the taxpayer retains property, business interests, or family in the origin state. The burden of proof on the taxpayer to establish a clean break is real.

The practical checklist for an actual domicile change is well-developed and a competent CPA will walk you through it: voter registration, vehicle registration and titling, drivers license, primary medical care, dependent residency, professional licenses, time-tracking documentation, and the formal severing of indicia in the origin state. None of this is exotic; all of it requires intention and follow-through.

## Rental income, vacation-home rules, and IRS treatment

The federal tax treatment of a Scottsdale second home turns substantially on whether and how you rent the property. The headline categories under IRC §280A are:

A property you do not rent at all is a personal-use second residence. Mortgage interest and property tax may be deductible up to the federal caps that apply to second homes. This is the typical lock-and-leave model.

A property you rent for 14 days or fewer per year and use personally for the rest is also treated as a second residence, and the rental income is not federally taxable. The "14-day rule" is well-known and is a useful structure for buyers who want to host a single family event or occasional vacation rental without triggering rental-property treatment.

A property you rent for more than 14 days per year and use personally substantially is a mixed-use vacation rental, and the tax treatment becomes meaningfully more complex: deductions are limited, income is taxable, and the proper allocation between personal and rental use is a CPA-supervised calculation.

A property you rent commercially and do not use personally is investment real estate. This is rare in the Scottsdale luxury second-home segment because most prestige communities prohibit short-term rentals via the CC&Rs.

## Common mistakes that increase tax bills unnecessarily

**Not getting CPA advice in both states before transacting.** The largest single avoidable expense we see is buyers who structure a purchase or a residency transition without coordinated advice from a CPA who understands both the origin state\u2019s departure rules and Arizona\u2019s residency rules. A few thousand dollars of CPA time before transacting saves multiples of that over an ownership lifecycle.

**Assuming residency change is automatic.** Spending several months a year in Scottsdale does not change your tax residency. The indicia of domicile must be moved deliberately, and the timing of the move matters. Several origin states audit residency claims for high-net-worth taxpayers, and a poorly-documented transition can result in continued tax exposure for several years.

**Renting the home short-term in violation of CC&Rs.** Most Scottsdale luxury private communities prohibit rentals under 30 days. Some prohibit any rental. Renting in violation of the CC&Rs is both a community violation and potentially a city short-term-rental-ordinance violation. The financial upside rarely justifies the legal exposure.

**Holding the property in personal name when an entity structure would be more tax-efficient.** For very-high-net-worth buyers, the question of entity structuring (LLC, trust, partnership) can have meaningful federal tax and estate-planning consequences. This is not a one-size-fits-all decision: talk to your tax attorney and CPA before assuming the default of personal-name ownership is right for your situation.

**Missing the property-tax appeal window.** Maricopa County publishes valuation notices annually and provides a defined administrative appeal window. For a high-value property, a successful appeal can produce meaningful annual savings that compound over the holding period. Many out-of-state owners miss the appeal window simply because the notice arrives at the Scottsdale address while they are at their primary residence. Have a local property manager or attorney monitor valuation notices on your behalf and flag any year-over-year increase that appears unsupported by comparable assessments.

**Failing to coordinate Scottsdale ownership with estate planning.** A Scottsdale home held in personal name passes through Arizona probate at death: a process that is public, time-consuming, and avoidable. Most luxury buyers can hold the property in a revocable trust or other estate-planning vehicle that bypasses probate entirely. The cost of setting up the appropriate structure at acquisition is materially lower than the cost of restructuring later or letting the estate work through probate.
