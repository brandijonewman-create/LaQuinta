Joining an equity country club is not just buying access to a golf course. It is buying a share of an organization that you and the other members own and pay for. That is the appeal of the equity model, and it is also the risk. If the club is well run and well funded, members benefit. If it is underfunded or overextended, members pay.

This guide explains how to check an equity club’s financial health before you join. For the basics of how equity memberships work, see our [guide to how equity golf memberships work](/blog/what-is-an-equity-golf-membership).

*Important: This is general editorial information, not legal, tax, accounting or financial advice. Every club is different, and its documents control. Review a club’s financial and governing documents with a licensed accountant, attorney or financial professional before you join.*

## Why a club’s finances matter to you

At an equity club, three things connect your money to the club’s finances.

**Dues and assessments.** The board sets dues each year and can levy assessments for major projects or shortfalls. A club that has not saved for big repairs is more likely to ask members for money.

**Your initiation.** At many equity clubs, part of your initiation may be returned when your membership is resold, depending on the documents. That refund often depends on demand for memberships, which depends on the club’s condition and reputation.

**The club’s future.** A financially healthy club can invest in its course and facilities. A struggling one may defer maintenance, cut services or, in extreme cases, face restructuring.

## Step 1: Ask for the right documents

Ask the club for:

1. **Audited financial statements** for at least the last three years
2. **The current operating budget**
3. **The capital reserve study**, which estimates when major assets will need replacing and what that will cost
4. **Any long-range capital plan or campus master plan**
5. **The bylaws and membership plan**, including assessment and refund rules
6. **Two to three years of board minutes**, which often reveal issues before they reach the financial statements
7. **Membership statistics**: current members by category, waitlist length, resignations and new members over several years

Many equity clubs share financial information with serious prospective members, sometimes after an application or under a confidentiality agreement. If a club will not share basic information at all, weigh that carefully.

## Step 2: Read the financial statements

You do not need to be an accountant to spot the big themes, though you should have one review the details. Industry sources point to several warning signs.

**Declining net worth.** If the club’s equity is shrinking year after year, it is consuming more than it is building.

**A shrinking capital base.** If the value of the club’s facilities is falling because it is not reinvesting, the course and clubhouse will eventually need major spending.

**Dues under half of revenue.** One industry benchmarking group flags clubs where dues make up less than half of total revenue, since dues are the most stable income a club has.

**Capital charges that do not keep pace.** If the money the club raises for capital does not cover depreciation and debt service, it is falling behind on its long-term needs.

**Benchmarking.** Some accounting firms publish benchmarks built from the audited reports of hundreds of private clubs. RSM, for example, publishes an annual report on financial and operating trends in private clubs drawn from more than 200 clubs. Comparing a club against benchmarks like these can put its numbers in context.

## Step 3: Look at debt

Clubs borrow for big projects such as new clubhouses or course renovations. Debt is not automatically bad, but too much limits a club’s options.

- **Ask what the debt funded** and whether that project is complete.
- **Ask how it will be repaid**, whether from dues, a dedicated capital charge or an assessment.
- **Ask about the ratio of debt to equity.** One golf-club adviser suggests 50% or less is healthy and treats anything over 100% as a red flag. Standards vary, so ask your accountant how this club compares.

## Step 4: Check reserves and the capital plan

Every club eventually has to replace roofs, irrigation systems, bunkers, greens, kitchens and equipment. A healthy club plans and saves for this.

- **Is there a current reserve study?** When was it last updated?
- **How well funded are the reserves** compared with what the study says the club needs?
- **Is there a long-range capital plan?** What major projects are expected in the next five to ten years, and how will they be paid for?

Some clubs set aside part of every initiation for capital needs. Ask whether this club does.

## Step 5: Look at assessments

Capital assessments are common at private clubs, and they are often contentious. Ask:

- How many assessments have members paid in the last ten years, and how large were they?
- Are any assessments planned or under discussion now?
- How are assessments approved: by the board or by a member vote?
- Can assessments be paid over time?

A history of frequent, large assessments can signal that the club has not been saving enough. A club that funds capital needs through regular, predictable charges is usually easier to budget around.

## Step 6: Check membership trends

A club’s finances depend on its members. Ask for several years of figures on:

- **Total members by category**
- **New members and resignations each year**
- **The waitlist**, if there is one, and how long it has been
- **The resale queue**, if refunds depend on resales, and how long resigned members wait

A long waitlist suggests strong demand. A long resale queue, or a falling member count, suggests the opposite. Both affect your future costs and any refund you may receive.

## Step 7: Read the board minutes

Board minutes often show problems before they reach the financial statements: debates over deferred maintenance, rising costs, disputes about assessments, or legal matters. Read two to three years of minutes, and note anything that comes up repeatedly.

## Questions to ask the club

1. What do the last three years of audited statements show about net worth and cash flow?
2. How much debt does the club carry, what did it fund and how will it be repaid?
3. When was the reserve study last updated, and how well funded are the reserves?
4. What major projects are planned, and how will they be paid for?
5. How many assessments have members paid in the last ten years?
6. How have membership numbers, the waitlist and the resale queue changed?
7. How are dues increases decided, and what has the history been?

## Governance: who is minding the money

Numbers tell you where a club stands. Governance tells you where it is likely to go. Ask:

- **How is the board elected,** and how long do directors serve?
- **Is there a finance committee,** and does it include members with financial experience?
- **Who audits the club,** and has the auditor raised any concerns?
- **Who manages day to day:** a general manager employed by the club, or a management company?
- **How does the club communicate with members** about budgets, projects and assessments?

A club with experienced volunteers, an independent audit and regular, plain-language reporting to members is generally easier to trust with your money.

## Red flags to take seriously

Any one of these deserves a careful follow-up question:

- The club cannot or will not share audited statements
- Net worth has fallen for several years in a row
- Large projects are planned with no clear funding plan
- Assessments have been frequent and large
- Membership numbers are falling while the resale queue grows
- Board minutes show repeated, unresolved disputes about money
- Deferred maintenance is visible on the course or in the clubhouse

## What a healthy club tends to look like

There is no single profile, but financially healthy equity clubs often share some traits: stable or growing membership, dues that make up a large share of revenue, a current reserve study with reserves funded to plan, moderate debt with a clear repayment plan, predictable capital charges rather than frequent surprise assessments, and transparent communication with members.

## How this applies in Scottsdale

Several well-known Scottsdale-area clubs are member-owned, according to public sources, including Desert Mountain Club, Mirabel, The Estancia Club, The Country Club at DC Ranch and Tonto Verde. Desert Mountain is a notable example of members taking control: in 2011, members bought the club’s six golf courses, related facilities and about 500 acres of developable land from Crescent Real Estate Holdings for a reported $73.5 million. If you are considering any equity club, ask for its documents and apply the steps above.
