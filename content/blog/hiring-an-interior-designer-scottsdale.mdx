A Scottsdale golf home asks a lot of its interiors. The light is brilliant for most of the year, the views are often the point of the house, and many owners live here only part of the year. A good interior designer turns those conditions into rooms that look right in January and still work in July.

This guide covers what to know before you hire one: what the title does and does not mean in Arizona, how designers charge, what desert living asks of a design, and how to structure the engagement.

*Important: This is general editorial information, not legal or financial advice. Rules, credentials and fees change. Verify requirements and costs for your project with the relevant agency and a licensed professional before you make a decision.*

## What the title means in Arizona

About two dozen U.S. jurisdictions regulate interior designers in some form. Arizona is not one of them. The state does not require licensure or registration to practice as an interior designer, which means anyone can use the title.

That is not a reason for concern so much as a reason to ask better questions. Since the title alone does not tell you about training, look at:

- **Education.** A degree in interior design, ideally from an accredited program.
- **NCIDQ certification.** The national certification administered by the Council for Interior Design Qualification, earned through education, experience and an exam. It is voluntary in Arizona and widely recognized as a professional standard.
- **Professional memberships.** Organizations such as the American Society of Interior Designers (ASID) have membership categories tied to education and experience.
- **Completed work.** The most useful credential of all is a portfolio of finished homes like yours, with clients willing to talk about the experience.

Some designers focus on decorating: furniture, art, textiles and styling. Others work on space planning, lighting plans and finish selections for new construction and remodels. Both are valuable. Make sure the designer’s strengths match your project.

## Design, construction and permits

When a project moves walls, plumbing, electrical or structure, the designer plans it and a contractor builds it. In Arizona, contractors are licensed by the Arizona Registrar of Contractors, and remodeling generally falls under the B-3 General Remodeling and Repair classification. Structural, plumbing, mechanical and electrical work usually needs a city permit.

If your designer will manage the construction side, ask:

- Which contractor will do the work, and what is its license number?
- Who pulls the permits?
- Who is responsible if the work fails inspection?

In gated golf communities, exterior changes such as windows, doors, lighting and paint colors usually need approval from the community’s design review committee as well. Interior work usually does not, but confirm with your community before you plan anything that touches the exterior.

## How designers charge

There is no standard fee in the industry, and the structure matters as much as the rate. These are the common models:

**Flat design fee.** One price for a defined scope, such as a full design plan for the main living spaces. Clear and predictable, as long as the scope is clearly written.

**Hourly rate.** You pay for the time spent. This suits smaller projects and consultations. Ask for an estimate of total hours and how often you will see a time report.

**Percentage of the project.** The fee is a percentage of the total project cost. Common on large new builds and full renovations. Ask exactly what the percentage applies to.

**Markup on purchases.** The designer buys furniture, fabric and materials at trade pricing and charges you a markup. Ask whether the markup is applied to the trade price or the retail price, and whether you will see the original invoices.

**Combination.** Many designers charge a design fee plus a markup on purchases, or an hourly rate plus a markup.

Rates vary widely by designer, scope and city, so we do not quote a single figure. When you compare proposals, estimate the **total** expected cost of each one, fees and purchases together, for the same scope. *Verify fees and terms with each designer before you sign.*

## Designing for the desert

Scottsdale’s climate shapes what lasts and what looks right. A designer with local experience will raise these points before you do.

### Light and fading

Sunlight here is intense, and south- and west-facing rooms take the most of it. Fabrics, rugs, wood finishes and art can fade faster than buyers from other climates expect. Ask about:

- Performance fabrics and fade-resistant materials for sunny rooms
- Window treatments that control glare and heat without blocking the view
- Placement of valuable art away from direct sun, and UV protection for glass where needed

### Heat and indoor-outdoor living

Many Scottsdale golf homes are designed around patios, outdoor kitchens and disappearing glass walls. Outdoor furniture and fabrics need to stand up to sun and heat, and indoor and outdoor spaces should feel like one design when the doors are open. If your home faces a fairway, think about how the view reads from inside, and how much privacy you want from the course.

### Dust and maintenance

Desert dust finds its way into homes, especially in spring and during monsoon season. Low-maintenance finishes and easy-to-clean surfaces are a practical choice, not a compromise.

### Part-year living

If you spend summers elsewhere, design for the home you leave and the home you return to:

- Choose materials that tolerate a closed-up house in summer
- Plan storage for seasonal items
- Ask the designer to coordinate with your home watch or property manager on how the home is set up when you are away

## Matching style to architecture

Scottsdale’s golf communities hold a wide range of architecture, from Santa Barbara and Spanish Colonial to desert contemporary and modern. An interior that fights the architecture rarely looks right. A good designer reads the house first: its materials, its lines and its relationship to the landscape.

## The interview checklist

Bring these questions to your first meetings:

1. **Background.** What is your training, and do you hold NCIDQ certification or professional memberships?
2. **Portfolio.** Can we see completed homes in the Scottsdale area, ideally similar in size and style to ours?
3. **References.** Can we speak with two recent clients?
4. **Scope.** Which services are included: space planning, lighting plans, finish selections, furniture, art, installation?
5. **Fees.** How is each part of the bill calculated, in writing?
6. **Purchases.** Do purchases go through you, and will we see the original invoices?
7. **Schedule.** What is the realistic timeline, including lead times for major pieces?
8. **Contractors.** If construction is involved, who builds it and what is that contractor’s ROC license number?
9. **Communication.** How will decisions be presented, and how quickly do you need answers?
10. **Changes.** How are changes to scope or selections priced?
11. **Remote owners.** How do you work with clients who are away for part of the year?
12. **Installation.** Who receives, inspects and installs furniture, and who handles damaged items?

## Prepare before the first meeting

Designers do their best work when clients arrive with clear answers. A little preparation also makes it easier to compare proposals fairly. Before you meet anyone, settle these points with everyone who will live in the home:

- **How you use the house.** Full-time or part of the year? Hosting golf groups and family, or quiet weeks for two?
- **Which rooms matter most.** Many owners start with the great room, kitchen and primary suite, then phase the rest.
- **What stays.** List the furniture, art and heirlooms you want to keep, with photos and measurements.
- **Your budget range.** Share it early. A designer cannot plan a realistic scope without it.
- **Your deadline.** A holiday, a season opening or a guest visit can shape the schedule, and lead times shape what is possible by then.
- **Images you love, and images you don’t.** Both help a designer understand your taste quickly.

Bring the floor plan if you have one, along with photos of the rooms at different times of day. In a home with big desert views, the way light moves through the rooms is part of the design brief.

## What to put in the agreement

A clear written agreement protects both sides. At a minimum, it should cover:

- The scope of services, room by room
- The fee structure and payment schedule
- How purchases, deposits and markups work
- Who owns the design drawings
- How changes, delays and damaged items are handled
- How either side can end the agreement

Have an attorney review it if the project is large.

## The bottom line

In Arizona, the best signal of a strong interior designer is not the title but the work: finished homes in this climate, clients who would hire them again, and a fee structure you can explain to someone else in two sentences.
