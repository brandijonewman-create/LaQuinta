## How to use this guide

La Quinta holds one of the deepest concentrations of championship private golf real estate on the West Coast. Roughly a dozen named golf communities and seven fully-private clubs sit inside one ZIP code. The goal of this guide is to help you decide where you belong before you tour.

We move through it in five sections: (1) the seven private clubs that define the upper market, (2) the broader community spectrum, (3) architect lineage and why it matters, (4) the membership economics that buyers consistently underestimate, and (5) the seasonal rhythm of buying here.

## 1. The seven private clubs

**PGA West** (condos to $15M+). Six 18-hole courses by Pete Dye, Jack Nicklaus, Greg Norman and Tom Weiskopf — three private, three with resort access. The largest and most flexible of the seven, with multiple membership tiers and the widest housing stock in La Quinta.

**The Madison Club** ($4M–$30M+). Tom Fazio routing. Membership tightly held, financials private. The most discreet enclave in the valley. Buyers here are typically second- or third-home owners trading up from coastal California or the mountain west.

**The Hideaway** ($2M–$10M). Two routings (Pete Dye and Clive Clark) behind a single gate. Architecturally one of the most cohesive private communities in the valley — every home built within tight guidelines.

**Andalusia Country Club** ($1.4M–$6M). Rees Jones design at the southern edge of La Quinta. Modern Mediterranean architecture, big mountain views, newer construction.

**The Tradition Golf Club** ($2M–$12M). Arnold Palmer's only Coachella Valley design. Mature streets, tight membership, lower public profile than the flagship clubs.

**La Quinta Country Club** ($700K–$4M). Founded 1959, member-owned since 1977. Lawrence Hughes routing. Hosted the early Bob Hope Classic. The historic club of the valley.

**The Quarry at La Quinta** ($3M–$15M). A second Fazio routing on a dramatic granite-quarry site — one of the smallest memberships in the valley.

## 2. The broader spectrum

Beyond the seven private clubs sit additional named golf communities, ranging from semi-private clubs to active-adult communities. These are the workhorses of the La Quinta market — broader access, more inventory, more turnover, lower price points.

- **Rancho La Quinta Country Club** ($650K–$3.5M) — 36 holes by Robert Trent Jones Jr. and Jerry Pate.
- **La Quinta Resort & Club** ($500K–$3M) — the historic 1926 resort core.
- **Mountain View Country Club** ($900K–$3.5M) — Palmer-designed semi-private.
- **The Citrus Club** ($650K–$3M) — a Pete Dye design.
- **Trilogy at La Quinta** ($450K–$1.4M) — 55+ active adult on the Cove side.
- **Coral Mountain Club** ($1.5M–$7M) — small Nicklaus enclave.
- **Duna La Quinta** ($550K–$2M) — condos and casitas on the Dunes Course.

## 3. Architect lineage

The quickest way to triangulate fit is by architect. Pete Dye courses reward strategic play and punish width. Fazio courses emphasize visual drama and ground-game softness. Palmer is gentler. Rees Jones leaves distinct fingerprints — big fairways, penalizing rough, generous greens.

## 4. The membership economics buyers underestimate

The sticker price of a La Quinta home is rarely the full cost. Most private clubs require either equity membership (you buy in, you sell out when you leave) or non-equity membership (capital contribution + monthly dues, non-refundable).

- **Equity clubs:** Initiation deposit is large but at least partially refundable. Annual dues typically $20K–$45K. Membership often transfers with the home, but not always automatically — confirm with the club office before offer.
- **Non-equity clubs:** Smaller capital contribution, dues $15K–$30K annually, nothing refundable.
- **HOA dues** are separate from club dues — typically $400–$1,500 per month.
- **Property taxes** in Riverside County run roughly 1.1% effective on assessed value, plus Mello-Roos in some newer communities.

## 5. The seasonal rhythm

- **Jan–Apr (high season):** Showings are easy, inventory at peak, prices firm. The best time to *see* the lifestyle. The worst time to negotiate.
- **May–Sep (low season):** Triple-digit heat, snowbirds gone, inventory soft. The best time to *make an offer*.
- **Oct–Dec (shoulder):** The value sweet spot for primary residents.
